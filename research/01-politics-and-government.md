# Domain research: Politics & government

> How politics briefs the principal — the person at the podium did not do the research, but they carry sole accountability for it and will be interrogated on it live. Techniques, mechanics, why each works, and sources.

---

## 1. The Ministerial Submission (UK Civil Service)

**Where used:** Every UK government department, daily, for every decision a minister must own.

**Mechanics.** A submission is a 2–3 page structured document from officials: why the minister is reading this, what is recommended, background, and a "handling" or "presentation" section — with detail pushed to annexes. Crucially, civil service guidance says a good briefing gives "key points that need to be made" and then adds *defensive material*: "areas of vulnerability and the counter arguments" ([civilservant.org.uk/skills-briefing](https://www.civilservant.org.uk/skills-briefing.html), [civilservant.org.uk/skills-submissions](https://www.civilservant.org.uk/skills-submissions.html)). The Institute for Government notes a well-regarded submission "tells ministers very quickly why they are reading the advice and what is recommended" ([IfG on submissions](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/publication/submission-work-special-adviser)).

**Why it works.** It separates *decision*, *evidence*, and *defence* into distinct layers. The minister doesn't absorb everything; they absorb the recommendation, the reasons, and where the argument is weakest. Knowing your vulnerabilities in advance is treated as more important than knowing every fact.

## 2. The Red Box Ritual: Forced Nightly Engagement

**Where used:** All UK ministers.

**Mechanics.** Every evening ministers receive a locked red despatch box of submissions and briefs — 30–50 items a day at peak — which they are expected to work through overnight and return with decisions and marginal comments. The private secretary curates ruthlessly: material lands at the private office first, and only "box-worthy" items with a "compelling, concise, politically astute cover note" get through ([PoliMonitor, "Unwrapped: behind the ministerial red box"](https://www.polimonitor.com/blog/unwrapped-behind-the-ministerial-red-box); [IfG on private offices](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/explainer/ministers-private-offices)).

**Why it works.** Two things. First, a *gatekeeper who knows the principal* filters and annotates, so attention lands where it matters. Second, the box forces the principal to physically mark up the material — approve, reject, query. Sending a submission back is itself a signal ("make this clearer, make it politically ready"). The principal's understanding is built through repeated small acts of judgement, not passive reading.

## 3. "Lines to Take" and Defensive Q&A Annexes

**Where used:** UK ministerial briefs, press office guidance, PMQs packs.

**Mechanics.** Alongside a positive script ("lines to take"), briefs carry a Q&A annex of anticipated hostile questions with approved answers — often marked "if asked," meaning the answer exists but is only deployed if the question comes. The News Grid system coordinates which lines run on which day across government ([Wikipedia: News grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_grid); [Peter Hyman on the No.10 grid](https://peterhyman21.substack.com/p/revealed-how-the-no-10-grid-strangles)).

**Why it works.** It converts an open-ended interrogation into a mostly-closed one. The principal never improvises a *position* — only the *wording*. "If asked" lines also encode a defensive posture: you don't volunteer weaknesses, but you are never caught without an answer to them.

## 4. PMQs Preparation: The Weekly Full-Dress Rehearsal

**Where used:** No.10 Downing Street, every sitting Wednesday.

**Mechanics.** Per the Institute for Government's detailed explainer ([IfG: PMQs](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/explainer/prime-ministers-questions-pmqs)):

- Civil servants build a **briefing pack**: background and draft answers for every MP on the order paper; where the question can't be discovered (opposition MPs), *scripts for all likely questions*.
- Departments contribute policy briefs during the week; special advisers add a **political section** with pre-prepared attack lines.
- The PM spends "a few hours in the evenings early in the week, and the whole of Wednesday morning" in prep — testing attack lines, writing jokes, and **role-playing**: an aide plays the Leader of the Opposition and "throws abuse" at the PM, anticipating both policy answers and political attacks ([HuffPost insider's guide](https://www.huffingtonpost.co.uk/entry/pmqs_uk_5afac213e4b044dfffb5c88b); [tutor2u](https://www.tutor2u.net/politics/blog/prime-ministers-questions-part-one)).

**Why it works.** The pack covers *content*; the role-play covers *pressure*. Most PMs call PMQs the most stressful part of the job, and the weekly deadline forces the whole of government to keep the PM's knowledge current — several PMs have said PMQs is how they stay on top of every department. The rehearsal surfaces the gap between "I have read the answer" and "I can produce the answer under attack."

## 5. Murder Boards (US Confirmation Hearings)

**Where used:** Supreme Court and cabinet confirmation prep; originally military/academic; also used for press-conference prep.

**Mechanics.** Around 20 members of the President's team sit as mock senators and pepper the nominee for hours with questions "designed to trip them up or elicit an unscripted response," under conditions simulating the hearing (hot lights, long table, hostile tone). Elena Kagan and Neil Gorsuch both did extensive murder boards; prep also includes "a huge cram-course" on the relevant law ([NBC: Kagan's murder boards](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna37854053); [NPR on Gorsuch prep](https://www.npr.org/2017/03/19/520558684/senate-democrats-lighting-the-grill-for-gorsuchs-confirmation-hearings); [Fox: Gorsuch's intensive preparation](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/gorsuch-enters-high-stakes-confirmation-hearing-after-intensive-preparation)).

**Why it works.** The operating theory, stated by practitioners: a nominee testifies for 12 hours, but "if they make one 10-second mistake, it's all anyone will ever know about them — that is what the murder boards are about." The goal is not knowledge transfer but *stress inoculation*: rehearsing composure so the worst question has already been survived in private.

## 6. Debate Prep: Briefing Books + a Sparring Partner Who *Becomes* the Opponent

**Where used:** Every US presidential campaign since Ford's first mock debate in 1976.

**Mechanics.** Months out, staff assemble thick briefing books covering the opponent's policies, personality, applause lines — down to logistics like camera placement and "the price of milk" (the retail-facts trap). Once studied, the candidate does full mock debates on a replica stage, cameras rolling, against a stand-in who mimics "every facet of an opponent — the same arguments, applause lines and even jokes." Bob Bauer played "Trump-worst" for Biden; specialists rotate through for policy deep-dives ([NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/2016/09/22/494901644/how-clinton-and-trump-are-preparing-for-the-first-presidential-debate); [NPR: stand-ins take the stage](https://www.npr.org/2012/09/30/162025715/to-prep-for-debates-stand-ins-take-the-stage); [CNN 2024](https://www.cnn.com/2024/06/20/politics/debate-biden-trump-preparations-president); [TIME: The Art of the Debate Stand-In](https://time.com/archive/6936350/the-art-of-the-debate-stand-in/)).

**Why it works.** Book study alone produces knowledge that collapses under adversarial timing. The stand-in makes the attack *specific and embodied*, so on the night the candidate is experiencing recognition ("I've heard this before") instead of surprise. The trivia layer (milk prices) exists purely to close off gotcha vectors.

## 7. The PDB Briefer: A Human Who Pre-Digests and Sits With the Principal

**Where used:** President's Daily Brief, delivered by a dedicated intelligence briefer.

**Mechanics.** The briefer starts around 1 a.m., masters "about 20 graduate-level essays on unrelated issues within three hours," selects what this particular customer needs, rehearses an oral delivery, then hand-carries the book and briefs in person — Oval Office, residence, or the back of a limousine — for ~15 minutes, taking questions and carrying follow-ups back to analysts. Bush treated it as "a dynamic conversation"; Ford pioneered direct contact with working-level analysts ([GovFacts: About the PDB](https://govfacts.org/government/federal/presidency/about-the-presidents-daily-brief/); [Spyscape: the 1 a.m. wake-up call](https://spyscape.com/article/the-presidents-daily-brief-the-1-a-m-wake-up-call-that-rules-d-c); [Wikipedia: PDB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President%27s_Daily_Brief)).

**Why it works.** The document is not the product; the *conversation* is. A live briefer lets the principal test understanding immediately ("why do we believe this? how confident are we?"), and the follow-up loop means gaps become tomorrow's brief. The briefer also rehearses — even the briefing gets briefed.

## 8. Press Secretary Prep: The Tabbed Binder and the Morning Gaggle-Anticipation Cycle

**Where used:** White House press office; UK No.10 lobby briefings.

**Mechanics.** Each morning the press team scans the news cycle, canvasses policy councils and agencies for approved guidance on every live story, and assembles a tabbed binder — one tab per topic, each with the approved line and fallback answers. The secretary references the binder openly at the podium ([WaPo on McEnany's binder](https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2020/07/17/kayleigh-mcenany-watch-so-organized/)); the press office also engages reporters beforehand about likely topics, and questions asked at the briefing feed back into how the President himself is briefed ([Quora, ex-staff answers](https://www.quora.com/How-does-the-White-House-Press-Secretary-prepare-for-a-briefing)). The spokesperson defends positions they didn't decide by owning the *authorized formulation* rather than the underlying decision.

**Why it works.** The binder makes the boundary of the spokesperson's authority explicit: what's in the binder is safe to say; anything else is "I'll have to get back to you" — which is an approved answer, not a failure. It legitimizes visible reliance on notes.

## 9. Media Training: Bridging, and Rehearsal Until Off-Message Is Unlikely

**Where used:** Government and corporate spokespeople everywhere.

**Mechanics.** The ABC method: **A**cknowledge/answer the question briefly, **B**ridge ("what's even more important here is..."), **C**ommunicate the key message. Practitioners stress the bridge "is not a dodge — it answers the question briefly and then redirects" and that overuse sounds evasive ([Media First on bridging](https://www.mediafirst.co.uk/blog/13-more-bridging-phrases-and-how-to-use-this-key-media-training-technique-well); [Action Group: ABC(D)](https://action-group.co.uk/the-art-of-bridging-how-to-use-the-abcd-method-in-an-interview/); [Throughline Group](https://www.throughlinegroup.com/2019/03/28/media-interview-bridging-stay-on-track/)). Standard training = message development, bridging, recorded interview simulation, delivery, crisis drills; one trainer claims three recorded mock interviews makes an off-message quote 4x less likely ([Shadow: media training guide](https://www.shadow.inc/resources/media-training)). Committee-witness trainers add: write out the 5–10 questions that scare you most, one per page, and build prep around those; rehearse with a "critical friend"; if you don't know, say so ([Parli-Training](https://www.parli-training.co.uk/preparing-evidence-how-to-prepare-for-a-select-committee-hearing-as-a-witness/); [Quorum on congressional testimony](https://www.quorum.us/blog/congressional-testimony/)).

**Why it works.** It gives the principal a *procedure* for the moment their knowledge runs out — a graceful exit ramp (brief answer → bridge → safe ground, or an honest "I'll find out") — so a knowledge gap never becomes a visible panic.

## 10. Speechwriting: Manufacturing Genuine Ownership

**Where used:** Presidential and ministerial speechwriting.

**Mechanics.** The Obama/Cody Keenan process: (1) before drafting, the writer sits with the principal and asks "what's the story we're trying to tell — and why are *you* the only person who can tell it?"; (2) the writer channels the speaker's established voice, cadence, and argument style; (3) the principal edits — Obama edited most of 3,477 speeches, sometimes crossing out full pages, and "could always take one of our drafts to a higher place"; (4) the writer sits in on rehearsals and adjusts for tone and pacing ([GBH interview with Keenan](https://www.wgbh.org/news/2022-10-26/what-was-it-like-to-be-obamas-speechwriter-cody-keenan-on-working-with-the-former-president); [Miller Center oral history](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-oral-histories/cody-keenan-oral-history); [Writer's Digest on presidential speechwriters](https://www.writersdigest.com/the-role-of-us-presidential-speechwriters)).

**Why it works.** Ownership is transferred through *editing*, not reading. The intent conversation before drafting means the ideas were the speaker's first; the mark-up pass means the words become theirs; rehearsal makes delivery muscle-memory. By performance time the question "did you write this?" is genuinely ambiguous — which is the goal.

## 11. Failure Modes: What Being Caught Out Actually Looks Like

- **Chloe Smith, Newsnight 2012:** junior Treasury minister sent to defend a fuel-duty U-turn she had learned about hours earlier; Paxman asked when she was told and whether she believed the policy — she "avoided the answer simply because she genuinely didn't know" ([LibDemVoice](https://www.libdemvoice.org/ldvideo-chloe-smiths-carcrash-fuel-duty-newsnight-interview-29172.html); [BBC](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/av/uk-politics-20096219)). Failure cause: *sent out on someone else's decision without the decision rationale.*
- **Diane Abbott, LBC 2017:** couldn't cost her own police-numbers policy live (£300,000 → £80m; 25,000 → 250,000 officers). She had done seven interviews correctly that morning ([BBC](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/election-2017-39775693)). Failure cause: *a single load-bearing number not truly internalized; fatigue exposes memorization vs. understanding.*

Practitioners' consistent diagnosis: the well-briefed principal knows the *reasoning and the weak points*; the poorly-briefed one has memorized *conclusions*. Memorized conclusions shatter at the first follow-up ("why?"), which is exactly the question interrogators are trained to ask.

## Transferable principles for humans presenting AI-generated content

1. **Brief the weaknesses, not just the content.** Every AI output handed to a human should ship with a "defensive annex": the 5–10 hardest questions, the known limitations, and approved answers — the civil-service submission model. The scariest questions, written out one per page, *are* the prep plan.
2. **Separate "lines to take" from "if asked" lines.** Give the presenter what to volunteer and, separately, what to say only under challenge. This bounds improvisation: they never invent a position, only phrasing.
3. **Ownership transfers through editing, not reading.** Follow the speechwriter loop: intent conversation *before* generation, mandatory mark-up pass on the draft (the human must cross things out), and rehearsal aloud. A human who has edited the AI's output can honestly claim it; one who has only read it cannot.
4. **Rehearse under adversarial conditions before any high-stakes exposure.** A murder board / mock-debate equivalent: someone (or an AI) plays the hostile interrogator with realistic pressure and follow-ups. The point is stress inoculation and surfacing the gap between recognition and recall — one 10-second failure outweighs hours of competence.
5. **Give the material a human (or interactive) briefer, not just a document.** The PDB lesson: understanding is built in the Q&A conversation about the brief, with a follow-up loop that turns today's gaps into tomorrow's briefing. For AI content: the presenter should interrogate the AI about its own output ("why this claim? how confident? what's the counter-argument?") before anyone else can.
6. **Make reliance on notes legitimate.** The press secretary's tabbed binder shows that visibly consulting structured, topic-tabbed material is compatible with credibility — provided the binder marks the boundary of authority and "I'll get back to you" is a pre-approved answer, not a defeat.
7. **Teach an exit procedure for the edge of knowledge.** Bridging (answer briefly → bridge → safe ground) plus an honest "I don't know, I'll find out" prevents a gap becoming a car-crash. Failure analysis shows disasters come from bluffing past the boundary, not from admitting it.
8. **Internalize reasoning, not conclusions — and flag load-bearing numbers.** Abbott and Smith failed on "why" and on single critical figures. Any brief should mark its 2–3 load-bearing facts for deliberate memorization and always carry the causal chain ("we recommend X *because*..."), since follow-up questions attack reasons, never summaries.
