# Domain research: Business

> How business prepares principals to present and defend work produced by their teams — to boards, investors, analysts, and clients. The through-line: the presenter doesn't need to have made the work; they need to have been made to *rebuild its reasoning* under simulated pressure before facing the real audience.

---

## 1. Earnings Call Prep: The Q&A Bank and the Mock Session

**Context.** Quarterly earnings calls are the purest version of the problem: a CEO/CFO must defend numbers produced by finance, IR and legal teams, in front of professional interrogators (analysts) with the transcript on the record forever.

**Mechanics.**
- IR teams budget roughly **4–6 hours per executive per cycle**: 1–2 hours of message development, a **2-hour mock Q&A session at least a week before the call**, then final review ([WeConvene checklist](https://weconvene.com/earnings-call-preparation-checklist-ir-teams/), [AMW guide](https://amworldgroup.com/how-to/media-train-executives-earnings-calls)).
- The core artifact is the **Q&A bank**: every plausible question, each with a drafted, legally-reviewed answer. Crucially, these are **"messaging frameworks, not scripts to be read verbatim"** — the executive learns the position and the evidence, not the sentence ([ACCESS Newswire](https://www.accessnewswire.com/blog/the-earnings-call-preparation-checklist-for-ir-teams)).
- Questions are *predicted*, not brainstormed: teams mine **competitor earnings-call transcripts, prior analyst reports, and the company's own past calls** to model what this specific set of analysts will ask ([Irwin best practices](https://www.getirwin.com/blog/the-best-practices-for-effective-earnings-calls)).
- Prep is cross-functional: IR leads, finance holds the numbers, legal reviews for disclosure risk — so answers are pre-cleared on multiple axes before the executive ever says them.

**Why it works.** Rehearsing the *hardest* questions out loud "has the highest return of any prep activity, because it surfaces inconsistent messaging and weak answers while there's still time to fix them" ([WeConvene](https://weconvene.com/earnings-call-preparation-checklist-ir-teams/)). Speaking an answer aloud is a different cognitive act from reading it; gaps that survive silent review collapse under voice.

## 2. The Murder Board: Adversarial Rehearsal

**Context.** Pentagon/NASA-origin technique, now used for congressional testimony, IPO roadshows, thesis defenses and pitch prep.

**Mechanics.** A committee of colleagues role-plays the hostile audience and subjects the presenter to **deliberately harsher questioning than the real event will contain** — probing assumptions, methodology, and weak seams. NASA credits Hans Mark at Ames (1969–77) with formalizing it as a panel that tries to "kill" a proposal before it goes forward ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_board), [Audacia Strategies](https://audaciastrategies.com/murder-board-not-bad-sounds-use-criticism-prepare-team/)). The presenter must answer live, in role, with no timeouts.

**Why it works.** It converts unknown-unknowns into known-knowns while the stakes are still zero, and it inoculates emotionally: the real Q&A feels easier than the rehearsal. The presenter also discovers *which* answers they can genuinely give versus which they were only borrowing.

## 3. Consulting: Pre-Wiring, the Dry Run, and Backup-Slide Culture

**Context.** MBB partners routinely present recommendations where analysts and associates did the modeling. Three interlocking rituals make this safe.

**Pre-wiring.** Before any major recommendation reaches a board or steering committee, the team runs "a structured programme of informal alignment work: individual conversations with key stakeholders, a pre-read document that places the core argument before the meeting, and an explicit understanding of where each decision-maker stands." Experienced consultants "never walk into a steering committee cold" ([Hazeldine, Medium](https://medium.com/@marybeth.hazeldine/the-stakeholder-briefing-how-mckinsey-pre-wires-approval-before-the-main-presentation-56c36e9ad5b3)). Objections are surfaced and answered one-on-one, so the formal meeting ratifies rather than litigates. Notably: the pre-read must be a *finished argument*, not a draft deck — incomplete material makes reviewers "spend their pre-meeting time forming opinions about the gaps rather than the argument."

**The dry run.** A full live run-through in role, with the team playing the client. The distinguishing rule: "in a rehearsal if you mess up, you redo it until perfect; in a dry run you adjust and move on" — it tests recovery, not polish ([Presales Collective](https://www.presalescollective.com/content/the-dry-run-a-true-secret-weapon), [Turpin Communication](https://www.turpincommunication.com/should-you-practice-or-rehearse-for-your-next-business-presentation/)). During review, seniors point at any element and ask **"so what?"** — anything that can't be connected to the slide's action title is cut ([Deckary](https://deckary.com/blog/pillar-consulting-presentations-guide)). This ruthlessly shrinks the surface area the presenter must defend.

**Backup slides / the appendix.** The appendix is not leftover data; it is **"strategically prepared answers to anticipated questions,"** each slide "mapped to a specific anticipated question from a specific committee member," designed to be "retrievable in seconds and readable while someone is talking." The sorting test: **"if a slide makes the case, it is front; if it only defends the case when asked, it is appendix"** ([Winning Presentations on the executive appendix](https://winningpresentations.com/executive-appendix-backup-slides/), [appendix slides](https://winningpresentations.com/appendix-slides-presentation/)). Building the appendix forces the team to *model the room* — who asks first, what they press on.

**Why it works.** Pre-wiring removes surprise from both sides. The dry run gives the partner reps at *speaking* the team's logic. The appendix lets a presenter answer a hard question with prepared evidence — the "I have a slide on that" moment is the single strongest credibility signal in a boardroom.

## 4. The Pyramid Principle / SCQA: Structure as a Defense System

**Context.** Barbara Minto (McKinsey) — *The Pyramid Principle*, the de facto grammar of consulting communication.

**Mechanics.**
- **Answer first**: the recommendation leads; everything below it exists only to support it. Groupings must be MECE, and each level of the pyramid answers the question the level above provokes ([StrategyU summary](https://strategyu.co/pyramid-principle-partone/), [ModelThinkers](https://modelthinkers.com/mental-model/minto-pyramid-scqa)).
- **SCQA** frames the opening: **S**ituation (facts the audience already accepts), **C**omplication (what changed), **Q**uestion (the one this raises), **A**nswer (your claim) ([antonov.com.au](https://antonov.com.au/scqa-framework), [Think Insights](https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/scqa-logic)).

**Why it works — and why it matters for a presenter who didn't write it.** A pyramid is a *map of anticipated questions*: each supporting layer is literally the answer to "why?"/"how?" asked of the layer above. A presenter who has internalized the pyramid can regenerate any part of the argument from the top down, instead of recalling slides in sequence. Content structured this way is defensible because attacking any branch never topples the top line — the presenter always knows which claim a question is aimed at, and what sits beneath it.

## 5. Amazon: Narratives, Silent Reading, and the FAQ Discipline

**Context.** Bezos banned PowerPoint from S-team meetings in 2004; proposals arrive as six-page prose memos, read silently for 20–30 minutes at the start of the meeting ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/04/23/what-jeff-bezos-learned-from-requiring-6-page-memos-at-amazon.html), [Lark overview](https://www.larksuite.com/en_us/blog/amazon-6-pager)).

**Mechanics.**
- Full prose with complete sentences — "slides can obscure weak thinking… logical gaps go unnoticed" between bullets, whereas narrative "forces the author to close every loop they had left open in their own thinking" ([SEA Bridge](https://www.seabridge.space/post/the-amazon-6-page-memo-writing-that-forces-clarity)).
- **The silent reading meeting** guarantees a shared, complete grounding: nobody discusses a document they haven't actually absorbed, and questioning starts from the whole argument, not a skim ([sixpagermemo.com on the Bezos–Fridman conversation](https://www.sixpagermemo.com/blog/jeff-bezos-lex-fridman-six-page-memo)).
- **The PR/FAQ** extends this: a mock press release plus an FAQ that deliberately **asks the hardest questions of itself**, split into external/customer FAQs and internal/business FAQs covering "the economics, risks and challenges." The FAQ is described as "a detailed description of the dragons you will need to slay" ([workingbackwards.com](https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/), [Commoncog](https://commoncog.com/putting-amazons-pr-faq-to-practice/)).

**Why it works.** Narrative is a *verification format*: it cannot be produced (or genuinely absorbed) without the reasoning being intact. For the presenter-who-didn't-write-it, reading a well-built narrative transfers the argument's causal chain, not just its conclusions — which is exactly what Q&A tests. The self-authored FAQ institutionalizes the murder board *inside the document*.

## 6. Completed Staff Work: The "Sign It As Your Own" Standard

**Context.** WWII-era U.S. Army doctrine (Provost Marshal memo, 1942), widely adopted in government and business ([govleaders.org](https://govleaders.org/completed-staff-work.php), [ASQ/Edwards PDF](https://asq.org/-/media/public/qp/principle-of-completed-staff-work.pdf)).

**Mechanics.** Staff must study the problem and present the solution "in such form that all that remains to be done… is to indicate approval or disapproval." Requirements: work out all details without bothering the chief; consult other staff; present **a single finished recommendation, not options or half-formed drafts**; and pass the doctrine's famous **final test**: *"If you were the chief, would you be willing to sign the paper you have prepared, and stake your professional reputation on its being right? If not, take it back and work it over — it is not yet completed staff work."*

**Why it works.** It puts the accountability transfer *inside the production standard*. The boss can sign because the staff already signed, reputationally. Note the direction of the obligation: the doctrine disciplines the **producer**, not the presenter — the artifact must arrive at a quality where ownership is safe to assume. This is the missing half of most AI-content workflows.

## 7. Board Prep and the Chief-of-Staff Briefing

**Context.** CEOs/CFOs facing boards; principals prepped by chiefs of staff for any high-stakes meeting.

**Mechanics.**
- **Rule #1 is "no surprises"** — anything controversial is socialized with individual directors before the meeting; pre-wire calls surface objections privately ([OnlyCFO](https://www.onlycfo.io/p/creating-a-productive-board-meeting), [Not Another CEO](https://notanotherceo.substack.com/p/how-to-conduct-a-board-meeting)).
- **Pre-reads**: short (≈5 pages, "draft then cut by half"), sent at least 48 hours ahead; prep starts ~14 days out to leave room for internal review and pre-wire calls ([Nasdaq governance](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/governance/board-meeting-preparation)).
- **The briefing memo**: chiefs of staff prepare a standard package — objectives, audience intelligence (who's in the room, what each person cares about, their likely stance), talking points, **anticipated questions with suggested answers**, and desired outcomes; confrontational meetings get expanded Q&A sections and rehearsal time ([Tom Moylan, "Writing a Killer Briefing"](https://tommoylan.substack.com/p/writing-a-killer-briefing), [Baums, Medium](https://medium.com/gr-blog/how-to-write-great-meeting-prep-briefings-ecda72b6c884)).
- Structured Q&A prediction beats brainstorming: questions organized **by category (strategy, financials, risk, people) and by questioner intent**, with per-question response frameworks, bridge statements, and explicit **red lines** (what must not be said) ([Winning Presentations Q&A briefing](https://winningpresentations.com/qa-preparation-briefing-document/), [checklist](https://winningpresentations.com/qa-preparation-checklist-executive/)).

**Why it works.** The briefing memo relocates the surprise from the meeting to the prep. Modeling *specific people's* likely questions makes prediction tractable — questions come from interests, and interests are knowable.

## 8. Media Training: Bridging and the Message House

**Context.** Spokespeople routinely deliver messages crafted by comms teams — the closest existing analog to "presenting words you didn't write."

**Mechanics.**
- **The message house**: one page — a roof (single umbrella statement), 3–4 pillars (key themes), a foundation of proof points under each pillar ([messagehouse.org](https://messagehouse.org/), [Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/message-house-framework/)). Small enough to memorize, deep enough to source any answer.
- **Bridging**: acknowledge the question, then transition to a core message ("What I can tell you is…"). It keeps the speaker inside prepared territory without stonewalling ([NASFAA](https://www.nasfaa.org/atp_media_training), [Shadow media training guide](https://www.shadow.inc/resources/media-training)).
- **Recorded mock interviews**, repeated: one practitioner metric claims spokespeople who complete ≥3 recorded mock interviews are ~4x less likely to go off-message ([Shadow](https://www.shadow.inc/resources/media-training)).

**Why it works.** Media training solves internalization explicitly: you don't memorize sentences, you internalize **2–3 messages plus their proof points**, then practice generating natural language from them under fire until it's automatic. The message house is a compression format for someone else's thinking.

## 9. Failure Modes: Readers vs. Defenders

Synthesis from practitioner sources: the #1 complaint from executives about presenters is that they "aren't prepared to answer the tough questions," and executives "evaluate the presenter's command of content, not just the content itself" — questioning starts immediately, not politely at the end ([Verdana Bold](https://www.verdanabold.com/post/presenting-to-executives-how-to-get-heard-get-buy-in-and-get-results), [Duarte](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-craft-and-nail-your-upcoming-executive-presentation/), [SlideModel](https://slidemodel.com/executive-presentations/)). The reader-not-defender is exposed by: not knowing the assumptions under a number, not knowing what was considered and rejected, being unable to say anything not on the slide, and losing pacing when interrupted. Defenders share a 4-part system: forecast questions, build Headline→Reason→Proof→Close answers, bridge, control pacing ([Winning Presentations](https://winningpresentations.com/how-to-handle-difficult-questions-presentation/)).

## Transferable principles for humans presenting AI-generated content

1. **The murder-board rule: rehearse aloud against a hostile questioner before the real audience.** The single highest-ROI ritual across every domain. AI translation: the AI that generated the content should interrogate the human — a mock Q&A where the model plays the skeptical board/client, harder than the real room will be.
2. **Frameworks, not scripts.** IR and media training both insist prepared answers are positions-plus-evidence to regenerate in your own words, never text to recite. Verbatim recall fails under pressure; internalized structure doesn't. Brief the human on claims and proof points, not sentences.
3. **Ship the Q&A bank / FAQ with the deliverable.** Amazon's PR/FAQ, the IR Q&A bank, and the chief-of-staff briefing all attach *anticipated questions with answers* to the work itself. AI-generated content should arrive with a self-authored "hardest questions about this document, answered" appendix — including "what did you consider and reject, and why."
4. **Structure the content as a pyramid so it can be defended top-down.** An answer-first hierarchy where each layer answers the "why?" of the layer above means the presenter can regenerate any branch on demand instead of recalling pages. Ask the AI for the pyramid (one top-line claim, 3–4 supports, evidence under each) as a separate briefing artifact — this is the message house for the deck.
5. **Front deck / appendix split by the "make or defend" test.** Minimize what must be presented (everything must survive "so what?"); maximize prepared defensive depth, each backup item mapped to a specific predicted question. "I have a slide on that" is manufactured in advance.
6. **Force a silent-reading moment: narrative verification before presentation.** The human must read a prose narrative of the argument (not skim slides) before presenting — because narrative transfers causal reasoning, which is what Q&A actually tests. If the AI can't render its output as a coherent 2–6 page narrative, the content isn't defensible yet.
7. **Completed staff work as the generation standard.** Hold the AI to the doctrine's final test: would *it* stake its reputation on this being right, in signable form? Practically: the AI must flag confidence levels, verify its own claims, and surface anything the human could be caught out on — the producer owes the presenter a safely-ownable artifact.
8. **Pre-wire: never let the first contact with a stakeholder be the meeting.** Circulate the finished argument early, model the specific audience (who will object, to what), and resolve objections one-to-one. The AI can help build the "audience intelligence" section — per-stakeholder likely questions and red lines — so the meeting ratifies rather than litigates.
