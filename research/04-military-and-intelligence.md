# Domain research: Military & intelligence

> How the military and intelligence communities transfer understanding, not just conclusions — so a principal can decide, present, and defend based on staff work they didn't do themselves. Techniques, mechanics, why each works, and sources.

---

## 1. Completed Staff Work (the 1942 Provost Marshal memo)

**Name and context.** "The Doctrine of Completed Staff Work" was drafted by Col. Archer J. Lerch, Deputy Provost Marshal General, U.S. Army, and published in the *Army and Navy Journal*, 29 January 1942. It defines the standard for what staff hand up to a chief. ([govleaders.org](https://govleaders.org/completed-staff-work.php), [technicalassent.com](https://www.technicalassent.com/insight/revisiting-the-doctrine-of-completed-staff-work))

**Mechanics.**
- Completed staff work is "the study of a problem, and presentation of a solution... in such form that all that remains to be done on the part of the... commander is to indicate his approval or disapproval of the completed action."
- The staff officer works out every detail themselves; they do not consult the chief on the details, "no matter how perplexing they may be." Half-formed ideas go to peers, not upward.
- The output form matters: "Writing a memorandum **to** your chief does not constitute completed staff work, but writing a memorandum **for** your chief to send to someone else does." The deliverable is the *signable action*, not a description of the problem.
- Advice, if given, is a single coordinated recommendation — not a menu of undigested options that pushes the thinking back onto the chief.
- **The final test:** "If you were the chief, would you be willing to sign the paper you have prepared, and stake your professional reputation on its being right? If not, take it back and work it over, because it is not yet completed staff work." ([goldstandardmanagement.org PDF](https://www.goldstandardmanagement.org/SupportResources/Articles/CompletedStaffWork.pdf))

**Why it works.** It defines a bright-line ownership transfer point: the chief signs, and by signing makes the staff's views *his own views*. The signature test forces the producer to pre-experience the principal's accountability. The direction of the doctrine is instructive for the AI case *in reverse*: the AI is the "staff," the human is the chief — so the human's job is not to redo the work but to apply the sign-it test honestly: *can I stake my reputation on this being right?* If not, it goes back for rework.

## 2. Military Briefing Formats: BLUF and the Four Briefing Types

**Name and context.** U.S. Army FM 6-0 (*Commander and Staff Organization and Operations*, Chapter 7) codifies four briefing types: **information**, **decision**, **mission**, and **staff** briefings. ([globalsecurity.org FM 34-80 App B](https://www.globalsecurity.org/intell/library/policy/army/fm/34-80/appb.htm), [USASMA briefing guide](https://rdl.train.army.mil/catalog-ws/view/100.ATSC/F736D308-72F2-4865-850C-8640EB48177C-1274442334203/usasma_w122/w122.htm))

**Mechanics.**
- **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front):** the conclusion and the ask come first; evidence follows. The audience knows within seconds what kind of cognitive act is being requested of them.
- **Information briefing:** facts only, explicitly no recommendation — the briefer announces this so the audience doesn't hunt for a hidden ask.
- **Decision briefing:** the briefer states *at the start* "I am seeking a decision," and *at the end* requests the decision.
- **Mission briefing:** issues or reviews an order, typically in the five-paragraph OPORD format.
- **Staff briefing:** coordination and shared situational awareness across staff sections.

**Why it works.** Typing the briefing separates *what I know* from *what I want you to do*, so the principal is never ambushed by an implicit ask. BLUF also front-loads the claim so that everything after it is heard as *evidence to be tested* rather than a narrative to be absorbed passively. Transferable directly: every AI-content handover should declare its type — "this is information" vs. "this needs your decision."

## 3. Confirmation Brief and Backbrief: Proving Understanding by Re-Briefing

**Name and context.** Army rehearsal doctrine (ADP 5-0; CALL Newsletter 98-5) distinguishes two receiver-side rituals. ([globalsecurity.org CALL 98-5](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/report/call/call_98-5_rehears4.htm), [CALL Handbook 19-18, *Commander and Staff Guide to Rehearsals*](https://api.army.mil/e2/c/downloads/2023/01/19/48e6a637/19-18-commander-and-staff-guide-to-rehearsals-a-no-fail-approach-handbook-jul-19-public.pdf))

**Mechanics.**
- **Confirmation brief:** *immediately* after receiving the order, the subordinate briefs back, in their own words: the higher unit's mission, the commander's intent, their own task and purpose, and their timeline. Misunderstandings are corrected *before* any planning starts.
- **Backbrief:** later, after the subordinate has planned, they brief the commander on *how* they intend to accomplish the mission — exposing their reasoning, not just their comprehension.

**Why it works.** Understanding is verified by *generation, not recognition*. You cannot re-express a plan in your own words, or derive a "how" from it, without a genuine internal model. The two-stage structure separately tests comprehension (confirmation) and reasoning built on that comprehension (backbrief). For AI content: a human who cannot backbrief the AI's output — restate its argument, derive its implications — hasn't yet earned the right to present it.

## 4. Commander's Intent: Transferring the WHY So Others Can Improvise

**Name and context.** A doctrinal element of every operations order: a concise statement of the operation's **purpose**, **key tasks**, and **desired end state**. ([smallwarsjournal.com](https://archive.smallwarsjournal.com/jrnl/art/disciplined-initiative-and-the-commander%E2%80%99s-intent), [armyupress.army.mil PDF](https://www.armyupress.army.mil/Portals/7/military-review/Archives/English/MilitaryReview_20131231_art011.pdf), [mca-marines.org](https://www.mca-marines.org/gazette/commanders-intent-easy-to-understand-tough-to-articulate/))

**Mechanics.** Written to survive contact: short enough to memorize, explicit about *why* the operation exists and what "done" looks like, deliberately silent on method. When the plan breaks — communications cut, situation changed — subordinates exercise "disciplined initiative": they improvise new actions that still serve the stated purpose and end state.

**Why it works.** A plan transfers *conclusions*; intent transfers the *generative rule* that produced them. Someone who holds the why can regenerate correct behavior under conditions the plan never anticipated. For AI content: the human presenter needs the "intent layer" of the artifact — the purpose, the key claims, the definition of success — because Q&A is exactly the "plan breaks on contact" moment. If they hold only the slides, they can recite; if they hold the intent, they can improvise honestly.

## 5. The PDB Briefer: Deep Preparation and Calibrated Language

**Name and context.** The President's Daily Brief is delivered orally by a dedicated CIA briefer who did not author most of its content — the closest existing analog to a human presenting others' analytic work. ([cia.gov — "A Day in the Life of a PDB Briefer"](https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/a-day-in-the-life-of-a-pdb-briefer), [govfacts.org](https://govfacts.org/government/federal/presidency/about-the-presidents-daily-brief/))

**Mechanics.**
- The briefer starts at midnight; by ~4 a.m. they have selected the pieces and begin *rehearsing aloud*.
- Because briefers have expertise in only one or two regions, the **authoring analysts come in at 5 a.m. to "pre-brief" the briefer** — a structured knowledge transfer from producer to presenter, in person, before the principal ever hears a word.
- The briefer must be able to explain the *argumentation* behind each assessment — tradecraft, sourcing, alternative views — and answer detailed follow-ups on the spot.
- A closed feedback loop: the briefer logs every question and skeptical reaction, is debriefed at 9 a.m., and returns next day with answers.
- **Calibrated uncertainty language:** since Sherman Kent's 1964 "Words of Estimative Probability," analysts map verbal expressions ("likely," "almost certainly") to numeric probability ranges; ICD 203 now standardizes a seven-tier scale and requires analysts to **distinguish underlying intelligence from analytic judgment** and to state *confidence levels* (how strong the evidentiary basis is) separately from *likelihood* (how probable the event is). The core discipline: mark clearly **what is known, what is assessed, and what is unknown**. ([globalsecurity.org — Kent's "Words of Estimative Probability"](https://www.globalsecurity.org/intell/ops/probability.htm), [Wikipedia: Words of estimative probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Words_of_estimative_probability), [factually.co on ICD 203](https://factually.co/fact-checks/politics/how-icd-203-intelligence-analytic-standards-confidence-levels-work-why-they-matter-1d3959))

**Why it works.** The pre-brief converts the presenter from a courier into a defender: they absorb not the text but the *reasoning chain*, precisely because follow-up questions probe the chain, not the text. Calibrated language prevents the classic failure of secondhand presentation — silently upgrading "possibly" to "certainly" in transmission. The known/assessed/unknown split gives the presenter safe, honest answers at the edge of their knowledge.

## 6. Red Teaming, Devil's Advocacy, Premortems, Murder Boards

**Name and context.** A family of adversarial-challenge rituals applied to plans and presentations before they face reality.

**Mechanics and lineage.**
- **Devil's advocate (the original):** the Vatican's *advocatus diaboli* — formally the Promoter of the Faith, an office established by Sixtus V in 1587 — was *required* to prepare in writing every possible argument against a canonization; no act in the process was valid without his presence, and he could force consideration of any objection. Dissent was made a mandatory procedural role, not a personality trait. ([newadvent.org](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01168b.htm), [britannica.com](https://www.britannica.com/topic/devils-advocate))
- **Military red teaming:** descended from Prussian *Kriegsspiel* and Cold War RAND/Naval War College wargaming; institutionalized when the Army founded the University of Foreign Military and Cultural Studies (2004) to train dedicated red teams that inhabit the adversary's logic and challenge planners' assumptions during course-of-action analysis. ([warontherocks.com](https://warontherocks.com/who-stress-tests-u-s-war-plans/), [apps.dtic.mil](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/AD1019046.pdf))
- **Premortem (Gary Klein, HBR 2007):** the team assumes the plan *has already failed catastrophically* and independently writes down reasons why. Prospective hindsight raises correct identification of failure causes by ~30% (Mitchell/Russo/Pennington, 1989); the whole exercise takes 20–30 minutes and legitimizes dissent by making it an assignment. ([HBR "Performing a Project Premortem" PDF](http://homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Performing-a-Project-Premortem.pdf), [Wikipedia: Pre-mortem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-mortem))
- **Murder board:** a panel that simulates the hardest possible questioning before a real board — used for Army instructor certification, promotion boards, and Senate confirmation prep; documented in a CIA memo as early as 1952. The panel deliberately asks tougher questions than the real audience will. ([Wikipedia: Murder board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_board))

**Why it works.** All four convert challenge from a social risk into a scheduled ritual. For the presenter specifically, the murder board does double duty: it stress-tests the content *and* forces the presenter to construct answers under pressure — the act that actually builds the internal model they'll need in the real room. You discover what you don't understand only when someone attacks it.

## 7. Rehearsal of Concept (ROC) Drill

**Name and context.** A rehearsal technique (CALL Handbook 19-18, *Commander and Staff Guide to Rehearsals*) in which leaders physically walk the plan on a terrain model. ([CALL Handbook 19-18 PDF](https://api.army.mil/e2/c/downloads/2023/01/19/48e6a637/19-18-commander-and-staff-guide-to-rehearsals-a-no-fail-approach-handbook-jul-19-public.pdf), [army.mil — 38th ADA ROC Drill](https://www.army.mil/article/245715/rehearse_to_refine_38th_ada_conducts_roc_drill), [domesticpreparedness.com](https://domesticpreparedness.com/articles/rehearsal-of-concept-from-army-to-civilian-use/))

**Mechanics.** A scaled terrain model is built; each leader physically steps through their unit's actions phase by phase, narrating aloud, in sequence and in relation to everyone else's moves. It is interactive — participants interrupt with questions, and friction points, gaps, and resource conflicts surface in the walkthrough rather than in execution. It is cheaper than a full-dress rehearsal but still embodied and social.

**Why it works.** Spatial, verbal, and social encoding at once: saying your part aloud, in sequence, in front of the people you depend on, converts a document into a shared mental simulation. Errors of interface ("I thought *you* covered that") appear precisely because each person must generate their own portion. For AI content: a talk-through of the deck — presenter narrating every slide aloud to a colleague, in order, answering interruptions — is a ROC drill for a briefing.

## 8. The Decision Briefing: Making the Commander Own the Decision

**Name and context.** The FM 6-0 decision briefing, and the course-of-action (COA) decision brief within the Military Decision-Making Process. ([globalsecurity.org FM 34-80 App B](https://www.globalsecurity.org/intell/library/policy/army/fm/34-80/appb.htm), [pptclasses.com decision briefing](https://pptclasses.com/decision-briefing/))

**Mechanics.**
- Opens by stating explicitly that a decision is sought.
- Body separates, in order: **assumptions** (labeled as such), **facts bearing on the problem**, the **evaluation criteria** used to screen and compare options, then each COA's advantages and disadvantages against those criteria.
- Ends with a specific recommendation — "state actions recommended; be specific; do not solicit opinion" — then asks for questions and **requests the decision**.
- The commander doesn't just pick; they interrogate criteria and assumptions, and their decision (often a modified COA) becomes *their* plan, announced in their own commander's intent.

**Why it works.** Showing the criteria, not just the ranking, lets the principal audit the reasoning rather than trust the conclusion — they can disagree with a weighting and reach a different answer, which is what genuine ownership means. Labeling assumptions gives the principal the exact list of things that, if wrong, invalidate the recommendation. The explicit "I request your decision" makes rubber-stamping socially impossible: silence is not approval.

## Transferable principles for humans presenting AI-generated content

1. **The signature test.** Before presenting, apply Lerch's 1942 test verbatim: *would I sign this and stake my professional reputation on it being right?* If not, it goes back to the AI for rework — the human is the chief, and presenting is signing.
2. **Backbrief before you brief.** Prove understanding by generation: restate the AI's argument in your own words to a colleague (confirmation brief), then explain *how you'd defend and apply it* (backbrief) — before facing the real audience. If you can't re-derive it, you don't own it yet.
3. **Get the pre-brief, i.e., interrogate the producer.** PDB briefers are pre-briefed by the authoring analysts on the reasoning chain, not the text. Equivalently: interrogate the AI about its own output — why this conclusion, what evidence, what would change it, what's the strongest counterargument — until you hold the argumentation, not the wording.
4. **Carry the intent, not just the slides.** Extract purpose, key claims, and end state from the artifact and memorize *those*. Intent is what lets you improvise correct answers when Q&A goes off-script — the moment the "plan" always breaks.
5. **Mark known / assessed / unknown, and calibrate your words.** Adopt the intelligence discipline: separate verified fact from AI judgment from open unknowns, use consistent probability language, and never upgrade the AI's "likely" to your "definitely" in transmission. State confidence (basis quality) separately from likelihood.
6. **Murder-board it.** Schedule adversarial questioning — human colleague or the AI itself in red-team mode — that is harder than the real audience. This both finds flaws and *builds* the presenter's model, because understanding is forged by defending under attack.
7. **Run a premortem on the content.** "It's six months later and this recommendation failed badly — why?" Ten minutes of prospective hindsight surfaces the failure modes the presenter must be ready to discuss, and inoculates against overconfidence in fluent AI prose.
8. **Structure the ask so the decision is owned.** Declare the briefing type up front (information vs. decision), show criteria and labeled assumptions rather than just conclusions, make one specific recommendation, and explicitly request the decision — so the principal audits the reasoning and the approval is theirs, never a rubber stamp.
