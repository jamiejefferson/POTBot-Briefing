# Domain research: Cognitive science & the AI ownership problem

> Why a human handed AI-generated content doesn't own it, and what reliably fixes that — the evidence base, mechanisms, and design implications.

---

## 1. The Illusion of Explanatory Depth (IOED)

**Finding.** Rozenblit & Keil (2002, *Cognitive Science*) showed people believe they understand how things work (zippers, bicycles, policies) with far more depth than they do. When forced to actually produce a step-by-step explanation, self-rated understanding collapses — and stays recalibrated afterwards. The illusion is strongest for *causal/explanatory* knowledge, weaker for facts and procedures. ([Rozenblit & Keil 2002](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1); [PDF](https://time.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/ioed_proofs.pdf_1.pdf); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth))

**Mechanism.** We confuse *familiarity and access* with *possession* of knowledge: we can see the parts and know where the answer lives, so we mistake recognition for command. Explanation is rarely demanded in everyday life, so the illusion goes unpunished until challenged.

**Implication for handover.** Reading a polished AI output is the perfect IOED trap: it produces maximal familiarity with zero causal knowledge. The single most reliable calibration tool is *forced generation of an explanation without the document in front of you*. A briefing process should make the human explain the work's logic aloud/typed before they're allowed to consider it "theirs" — the confidence drop itself is the diagnostic.

## 2. Self-Explanation, the Protégé Effect, and the Feynman Technique

**Finding.** Explaining material — to yourself or someone else — is one of the largest known learning effects. Chi's self-explanation research shows medium-to-large gains; a meta-analysis (Bisra et al. 2018) found *g* = 0.66 across 69 effect sizes. The protégé effect: merely *preparing to teach* improves recall and conceptual understanding versus preparing to be tested (Nestojko, Bui, Kornell & Bjork 2014); teachable-agent studies at Stanford show students learn more when teaching an agent than when studying for themselves. The Feynman technique operationalises this: explain in plain language, find the gap, go fix the gap. ([Stanford AAA Lab protégé effect paper](https://aaalab.stanford.edu/assets/papers/2009/Protege_Effect_Teachable_Agents.pdf); [self-explanation neuroscience study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475224000240); [Feynman technique evidence overview](https://whennotesfly.com/concepts/learning-science-knowledge/feynman-technique-learn-anything-faster))

**Mechanism.** Generating an explanation forces inference, gap-detection, and integration with prior knowledge; the teaching frame adds accountability and a mental model of the audience, driving deeper organisation of the material.

**Implication.** The handover should invert roles: the human doesn't receive a briefing, they *give* one. "Explain this deck back to me as if briefing your MD" recruits the protégé effect. The bot's job is to be the ignorant student ("play dumb"), not the presenter.

## 3. Retrieval Practice / Testing Effect

**Finding.** Roediger & Karpicke (2006, *Psychological Science*): being tested on material beats re-reading it for durable retention — 61% vs 40% recall after a week — even though re-reading *feels* better and wins on immediate tests. Retrieval also enhances subsequent learning (forward testing effect). ([Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x); [Retrieval-Based Learning: A Decade of Progress](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED599273.pdf); [forward testing effect](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3983480/))

**Mechanism.** Retrieval reconstructs the memory, strengthening storage, creating new retrieval routes, and binding content to new contexts. Re-reading only raises fluency/familiarity, which decays fast — and misleads: most learners choose re-reading anyway.

**Implication.** A handover that consists of the human re-reading the AI output (even twice, even "carefully") predictably fails a week later in the client meeting. The process must be *question-driven*: closed-book questions about the content's claims, numbers, and reasoning, spaced if possible (a short quiz now, a shorter one tomorrow).

## 4. Desirable Difficulties (Bjork)

**Finding.** Bjork & Bjork (1994 onward): conditions that slow and effortfully impair *performance during learning* — spacing, interleaving, testing, generation — improve long-term retention and transfer; conditions that make learning feel smooth produce the "fluency illusion," where ease of processing is misread as knowledge. ([Bjork Lab research overview](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/research/); [Metcalfe & Bjork chapter](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/metcalfe/PDFs/Metcalfe-BjorkVolSubmitFeb14Final.pdf); [teacher's guide](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/robert-bjork-teachers-guide-desirable))

**Mechanism.** Effortful processing forces deeper encoding and discrimination; retrieval strength (current accessibility) and storage strength (durability) are dissociable, and fluent input inflates the former while doing nothing for the latter. Caveat: difficulty is only desirable if the learner can succeed at it — arbitrary friction doesn't help ([when disfluency is not desirable](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13421-012-0255-8.pdf)).

**Implication.** A polished AI deliverable is a fluency machine — maximally easy to consume, minimally encoded. Deliberately add *productive* friction to the handover: make the human predict before revealing ("what do you think the top risk is?"), summarise sections from memory, and resolve deliberately-surfaced open questions. Never add friction the human can't succeed at (e.g., quizzing on trivia).

## 5. Generation Effect

**Finding.** Slamecka & Graf (1978): information you generate yourself — even partially, from a cue — is remembered far better than equivalent information you read. Recent HCI work extends this to AI writing: the MIT "cognitive debt" study found self-reported *ownership* of essays was lowest in the LLM group, and systems that anchor AI suggestions to the writer's own text (rather than replacing it) measurably improve writer agency and ownership. ([Slamecka & Graf 1978 summary](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zWvCEwYz4Uv1dMHXynq3H5w); [2025 conceptual replication in expository text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-025-00645-2); [AnchoredAI paper on writer agency/ownership](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.16128))

**Mechanism.** Generation forces retrieval from semantic memory and item-specific + relational processing; ownership follows effortful contribution. Note the replication literature: the effect is robust for discrete items, more fragile for whole passages — so the design lesson is *targeted* generation (choices, headlines, arguments), not making the human retype prose.

**Implication.** Move human contribution *upstream*: during drafting, the AI should force real decisions (pick between two framings, supply the key number, name the recommendation) rather than presenting a fait accompli. What the human decided, the human remembers and defends. Log those decision points; they become the skeleton of the briefing.

## 6. Cognitive Offloading and Automation Complacency in the GenAI Era

**Finding.** Four converging strands:

- **Classic automation research** — Parasuraman & Manzey (2010): reliable automation induces complacency (insufficient monitoring) and automation bias (uncritical acceptance), in both novices and experts, worst under multitasking load. ([Parasuraman & Manzey 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720810376055))
- **Microsoft/CMU CHI 2025** (Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch et al.; 319 knowledge workers, 936 real GenAI uses): higher *confidence in AI* predicts less critical thinking; higher *self-confidence in own skills* predicts more. Critical engagement shifts from doing the work to verifying and stewarding it — and workers skip verification when they feel unable to judge the output. ([CHI 2025 paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3706598.3713778); [Forbes summary](https://www.forbes.com/sites/charlestowersclark/2025/03/14/how-ai-changes-critical-thinking-new-microsoft-research-findings/))
- **MIT "Your Brain on ChatGPT"** (Kosmyna et al. 2025; 54 participants, EEG): LLM-assisted essay writers showed the weakest brain connectivity, lowest ownership, and *couldn't quote their own essays minutes later* — the authors call the accumulated deficit "cognitive debt." (Preprint; sample-size and methodology critiques exist and it should be cited as suggestive, not definitive.) ([arXiv 2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872); [critical commentary](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856))
- **Gerlich 2025** (666 participants): frequent AI use correlates negatively with critical-thinking scores, mediated by cognitive offloading; strongest in younger users. ([Societies/MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/15/1/6))

**Moderator — tutor vs ghostwriter.** Bastani et al. (PNAS 2025, ~1,000 Turkish students): unrestricted GPT access boosted practice performance 48% but *cut* exam scores 17% versus no AI; a guardrailed "GPT Tutor" giving hints instead of answers erased the harm. Same model, opposite outcome — the interaction design is the moderator. ([PNAS paper](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122); [Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/))

**Implication.** The danger isn't AI per se but *answer-delivery mode*. A briefing bot should behave like GPT Tutor (hints, questions, scaffolded reveals), not GPT Base (finished answers). And because low self-confidence suppresses verification, the process must explicitly license and equip the human to challenge the output.

## 7. Accountability Frameworks: "The Human Owns the Output"

**Finding.** A norm is crystallising across open source, enterprise governance, and commentary: a named human must be able to read, understand, and explain any AI-assisted work product. A 2026 study of OSS contribution guidelines found 74% of AI policies require human oversight; Selenium's policy states "You are the author. You must read, review, and understand all AI-assisted output before requesting review." ([arXiv study of contribution guidelines](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16706)). The BetterUp/Stanford "workslop" research (HBR, Sept 2025) quantifies the failure mode: 40% of workers receive AI output that masquerades as good work; each instance costs ~2 hours to deal with, and senders are judged less capable and less trustworthy — the fix framed as "pilots, not passengers." ([HBR article](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity)). TechPolicy.Press warns that nominal human-in-the-loop becomes "procedural theater" when the human can't genuinely evaluate the output ([TechPolicy.Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/ai-efficiency-can-undermine-accountability-even-with-humans-in-the-loop/)). Vibe-coding critiques make the same point for code: ownership migrates to intention-setting while comprehension evaporates, and accountability must sit with someone who understands the artefact. ([Tanium overview](https://www.tanium.com/blog/what-is-vibe-coding); [vibe coding & UX ownership study](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10652v1))

**Implication.** "Human reviews it" is not a standard; "human can explain and defend it under challenge" is. A briefing process is precisely the mechanism that turns paper accountability into real accountability — and it should end with an explicit sign-off act ("I can stand behind this") that means something because it followed a genuine test.

## 8. Elaborative Interrogation and Calibration

**Finding.** Dunlosky et al. (2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) — the definitive techniques review — rates practice testing and distributed practice as high-utility; elaborative interrogation (generating answers to "why is this true?") and self-explanation as moderate-utility with strong lab evidence; re-reading, highlighting, and summarising-without-training as low-utility. ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1529100612453266); [AFT plain-language version](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky); [elaborative interrogation guide](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/elaborative-interrogation-teachers-guide)). Key caveat: elaborative interrogation requires some prior knowledge to work — novices can't generate "why" answers from nothing. Calibration research (echoing IOED and the fluency illusion) shows judgments of learning made immediately over visible material are inflated; delayed, closed-book self-tests give accurate readings.

**Mechanism.** "Why" questions force integration of new claims with existing knowledge and expose unsupported assertions; testing provides the only trustworthy signal of what's actually known.

**Implication.** The bot's interrogation should be *why*-shaped ("why this recommendation and not X?", "why will the client push back here?") rather than *what*-shaped, and should adapt to the human's prior knowledge — scaffolding context first where the human is novice, because unanswerable "why" questions just produce frustration.

## Design implications

Evidence-backed rules for a briefing/handover process that leaves a human genuinely able to stand behind AI-produced content:

1. **Pull decisions upstream: make the human choose during drafting, not approve after.** Present real forks (framing A vs B, which number leads, what the recommendation is) and record the choices. Generated/chosen content is better remembered and owned than delivered content ([Slamecka & Graf](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zWvCEwYz4Uv1dMHXynq3H5w); [AnchoredAI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.16128)). The decision log doubles as the briefing skeleton.
2. **Brief by interrogation, not narration.** The default handover is closed-book questions the human answers, not a summary the human reads. Testing beats re-reading roughly 61:40 at one week ([Roediger & Karpicke](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)); re-reading a polished output is the fluency illusion in pure form ([Bjork](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/research/)).
3. **End with a teach-back, with the bot playing the ignorant student.** "Explain this to me as if I'm the client" recruits the protégé and self-explanation effects (*g* ≈ 0.66; [Bisra meta-analysis via protégé research](https://aaalab.stanford.edu/assets/papers/2009/Protege_Effect_Teachable_Agents.pdf)) and, per IOED, the human's confidence self-calibrates the moment the explanation runs dry ([Rozenblit & Keil](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1)).
4. **Ask "why", not "what" — and target the defensible weak points.** Elaborative interrogation drives deep processing ([Dunlosky et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1529100612453266)). Prioritise the questions a hostile stakeholder would ask: why this option, what was rejected, where's the weakest evidence.
5. **Operate as tutor, never ghostwriter, at briefing time.** Give hints, scaffolds, and progressive reveals rather than answers; the same model helps or harms depending on this one design choice ([Bastani et al., PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)).
6. **Force a prediction before every reveal.** "Before I show the pricing section — what do you expect it says?" Failed predictions are desirable difficulties that dramatically improve encoding of the correction ([Metcalfe & Bjork](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/metcalfe/PDFs/Metcalfe-BjorkVolSubmitFeb14Final.pdf)). Keep difficulties succeedable: scaffold novices with context first ([disfluency caveat](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13421-012-0255-8.pdf)).
7. **Surface the machine's uncertainty and rejected alternatives explicitly.** Complacency and automation bias thrive on confident, seamless output ([Parasuraman & Manzey](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720810376055)); trust-in-AI is the strongest predictor of not thinking critically ([Microsoft/CMU CHI 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3706598.3713778)). Ship every deliverable with "things I'm least sure of" and "options I considered and dropped" — assignments for the human, not footnotes.
8. **Build the human's self-confidence to challenge, not just their knowledge.** Workers skip verification when they feel unqualified to judge ([CHI 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3706598.3713778)). Give them the evaluation criteria and source trail, and explicitly frame challenge as the job ("pilots, not passengers" — [HBR workslop](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity)).
9. **Space it.** One micro-quiz at handover, one the next day or before the meeting where it will be defended. Spacing plus retrieval is the highest-utility combination in the literature ([Dunlosky et al.](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky)) and counters the "cognitive debt" pattern where AI-assisted content can't even be quoted minutes later ([Kosmyna et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)).
10. **Gate sign-off on demonstrated explanation, not attestation.** "I read it" is procedural theater ([TechPolicy.Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/ai-efficiency-can-undermine-accountability-even-with-humans-in-the-loop/)); the emerging norm — from OSS policy ([contribution-guidelines study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16706)) to enterprise governance — is that a named human can *explain* the work. Only release the deliverable as "briefed" once the teach-back and hostile-question round is actually passed; that pass is what makes the accountability real.

**One-line synthesis.** Fluent consumption feels like understanding but isn't; generation, retrieval, explanation, and challenge are the only mechanisms that transfer real ownership — so a briefing bot must be an interrogating tutor that forces choices, predictions, and teach-backs, never a narrator handing over a finished, frictionless answer.
