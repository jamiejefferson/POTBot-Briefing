# Domain research: Law

> How the legal world prepares people to testify, argue, or present under hostile questioning based on preparation work largely done by others. Techniques, mechanics, why each works, and sources.

---

## 1. Witness preparation vs. coaching (US practice, ethical line)

**Context.** US lawyers may — and are expected to — prepare witnesses intensively for cross-examination. The ethical line is between *eliciting what the witness genuinely knows* and *supplying answers*.

**Mechanics.**
- Review of the likely questions in advance, including hostile ones; mock direct and mock cross-examination sessions that "probe the witness's memory by referring to other known evidence or facts" ([Missouri Bar](https://news.mobar.org/witness-preparation-and-counseling-whats-ethical--and-what-crosses-the-line/); [Farris, "Prepping, Not Coaching"](https://farris.com/content/uploads/2019/04/20180921-Ethics_of_Witness_Preparation.pdf)).
- Document refresh: the witness re-reads the key exhibits, emails, and prior statements *before* being questioned so recall is grounded in the record, not reconstruction.
- Practice sessions rehearse **form** (pace, tone, composure under hostile framing) while leaving **substance** to the witness's own knowledge.

**Why it works.** Preparation converts second-hand or half-remembered material into genuinely held, checkable knowledge; the ethical boundary exists precisely because testimony that is merely memorised script collapses under probing. The prepared witness has *reconstructed understanding*, not a crib sheet.

## 2. UK witness familiarisation (R v Momodou): rehearse the *format*, never the *content*

**Context.** England & Wales draws a hard legal line: *R v Momodou* [2005] EWCA Crim 177 forbids coaching outright but *welcomes* familiarisation ([judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2005/177); [Bar Council guidance](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Witness-preparation-June-2023.pdf)).

**Mechanics.**
- Witnesses attend a course (e.g. [Bond Solon](https://www.bondsolon.com/witness-familiarisation/faqs/)) explaining courtroom process, roles, and cross-examination techniques, then undergo a **full mock cross-examination — but on invented case material bearing no similarity to their actual case**.
- Trainers are independent barristers/solicitors with **no personal knowledge of the case facts**, so they cannot contaminate the evidence.
- Individual oral feedback follows each mock cross ([Bond Solon training](https://www.bondsolon.com/witness-familiarisation/witness-familiarisation-training/); [Counsel Magazine](https://www.counselmagazine.co.uk/articles/within-boundaries)).

**Why it works.** It separates two failure modes: (1) panic at the *process* of hostile questioning and (2) not knowing the *substance*. Familiarisation inoculates against (1) — the adrenaline, interruption, leading questions, imputations of dishonesty — so that on the day, all cognitive capacity goes to substance. And by using unrelated facts, the person's actual answers remain authentically their own — which is what makes them robust.

## 3. The brief to counsel: engineered knowledge transfer to a stranger advocate

**Context.** In England & Wales and Australia, a solicitor who built the case over months hands it to a barrister who may argue it within days. The transfer artifact is the **brief to counsel**.

**Mechanics.** A good brief contains ([Geoffrey Hancy](https://hancy.net/resources/briefing-a-barrister/how-to-brief-a-barrister/); [Edward Greaves](https://egreaves.com.au/briefing); [Lawyer Accelerator](https://lawyeraccelerator.com.au/the-secrets-to-effectively-preparing-a-barristers-brief/)):
- A **narrative memorandum**: who the client is, what the dispute is, what counsel is being asked to do, and the perceived strengths/weaknesses.
- A **chronology** of events — practitioners repeatedly say "the value of a properly prepared chronology cannot be overstated."
- **Curated, indexed, numbered documents** in the same order as the contents page, with irrelevant material weeded out and gaps flagged.
- Explicit deadlines and open questions.

Counsel then rebuilds understanding actively: reading against the chronology, identifying the issues, and reorganising the material around the questions a judge will ask — not around how the solicitor filed it.

**Why it works.** The brief's quality "has a direct impact on the quality and timeliness" of counsel's advocacy ([LPLC Good Counsel guide](https://lplc.com.au/uploads/main/Resources/Practice-Risk-Guides/Good-Counsel-Barristers-PRG.pdf)). The key insight: the handover artifact is designed *for interrogation*, not for reading — chronology, numbered sources, and issue-framing let the receiver find any fact fast and trace every claim to a document. Counsel never presents from the brief; they present from their own reconstruction of it.

## 4. Moot courts and the SCOTUS preparation routine

**Context.** US Supreme Court advocates face a "hot bench" — near-continuous questioning on any part of the record from nine directions.

**Mechanics.**
- **Question anticipation at scale.** Chief Justice Roberts, as an advocate, prepared for "more than a thousand" possible questions, expecting to receive eighty to a hundred; he spent about **five weeks preparing for a 30-minute argument** ([De Novo / Virginia Appellate Law blog](https://www.virginiaappellatelaw.com/2011/05/articles/oral-argument/i-am-john-roberts-and-so-can-you-part-ii-oral-argument/)).
- **Random-order drilling.** Roberts reduced the case to four or five core points, wrote them on index cards, and **shuffled them**, practising delivering the argument starting from any point in any order — because judges will not follow your outline.
- **Formal moots.** Georgetown's Supreme Court Institute moots nearly every argued case: a panel of experienced practitioners plays the Justices in a dress-rehearsal courtroom, fires questions, then critiques ([Georgetown](https://www.georgetown.edu/news/moot-courtroom-us-supreme-court-prep/); [SCI FAQs](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/supreme-court-institute/moot-court-program/faqs-for-advocates/)). Advocates typically do multiple moots in the 2–3 weeks before argument, then isolate (often at a hotel near the Court) for final absorption ([ADF](https://adflegal.org/article/oral-argument-prep-supreme-court/); [SCOTUSblog on remote moots](https://www.scotusblog.com/2021/08/remote-moots-preparing-to-argue-at-the-supreme-court-by-phone)).
- **Answer-first technique.** For a hot bench: answer the question directly ("yes/no/it depends"), *then* bridge back to your affirmative points ([ABA, "Top Tips for Top-Notch Oral Argument Answers"](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/judicial/publications/appellate_issues/2022/winter/top-tips-for-top-notch-oral-argument-answers/)).

**Why it works.** The ratio matters: prepare 1,000 answers to deliver 100. The shuffled-cards drill destroys dependence on linear script — the advocate holds the argument as a *graph* they can enter anywhere, which is exactly what surviving interruption requires. Moots surface the weakest points before the real audience does, in a room where being wrong is cheap.

## 5. Deposition rules: a discipline for answering under hostile questioning

**Context.** Deponents (executives, clients) are taught a small set of behavioural rules that hold regardless of subject matter.

**Mechanics** ([ABA "Four Simple Rules"](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/young_lawyers/resources/tyl/practice-areas/deposition-preparation-four-simple-rules/); [US Legal Support](https://www.uslegalsupport.com/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-deposition-tips-for-preparing-clients/); [Talkov Law](https://talkovlaw.com/deposition-tips-traps-tricks/)):
1. **Listen to the entire question; pause** (some counsel teach a five-second pause) before answering.
2. **Answer only the question asked** — never volunteer. "What is your address?" gets one address, not a housing history.
3. **Never guess or speculate.** Guesses become admissions.
4. **"I don't know" and "I don't recall" are fully acceptable answers** when true.
5. Review the key documents beforehand so you are "not caught off guard," and ask to see any document before testifying about it.

**Why it works.** These rules acknowledge that under pressure, people fabricate fluency — they fill silence, guess, and over-explain, and every unforced word is new attack surface. The rules make *scope control* and *honest uncertainty* the trained default. Crucially, "I don't know" is reframed from failure into a safe, legitimate move — which paradoxically makes the witness more credible on what they *do* assert.

## 6. The Rule 30(b)(6) corporate designee: the purest analogue to the AI problem

**Context.** Under US FRCP 30(b)(6), an organisation must produce a witness to testify not to their personal knowledge but to **all knowledge "reasonably available to the organization"** — i.e., one human must absorb and stand behind work done by many others.

**Mechanics** ([ABA "Speak for Yourself"](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/tort_trial_insurance_practice/resources/brief/archive/speak-yourself-30b6-deposition/); [Trial Guides](https://www.trialguides.com/blogs/news/frcp-30b6-the-ultimate-guide-to-organizational-depositions-in-federal-litigation); [Markowitz Herbold](https://www.markowitzherbold.com/Preparing-and-Responding-to-the-Rule-30-b-6-Notice)):
- The company has an **affirmative duty to educate** the designee: review of documents, prior testimony, and files, plus **interviews with employees "from mailroom to senior management," even former employees**.
- A common two-session structure: session one gathers knowledgeable people to brief the designee; session two reviews everything collected and **confirms the corporate position** the designee will state.
- The designee testifies to the organisation's facts *and* its positions, beliefs, and interpretations — a synthesised institutional voice.
- **Failure is punished severely**: courts treat an unprepared designee "as a witness who never showed up," with fee awards, re-depositions at the company's cost, and even evidence preclusion ([LegalClarity](https://legalclarity.org/rule-30b6-depositions-notice-prep-and-sanctions/); [AGG/JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/be-thoughtful-in-your-rule-30-b-6-53426/)).

**Why it works.** The rule proves a person *can* legitimately testify to others' work — but only via a structured education process bounded by **noticed topics** (the questioner must list subjects in advance, and preparation targets exactly those). The scoping-plus-education pattern turns "I didn't do this work" from a disqualifier into a manageable engineering problem.

## 7. Expert witnesses: standing personally behind team-produced analysis

**Context.** Testifying experts routinely have staff run the models — yet under Daubert scrutiny, "your opinions are your own, and you need to be prepared to defend every single number" ([ACTEC podcast on valuation expert depositions](https://actecfoundation.org/podcasts/witness-depositions-of-valuation-experts/)).

**Mechanics.**
- The expert must have **genuinely reviewed and understood** anything they sign; adopting a report by signature without real review violates FRCP 26 norms ([Hofstra Law Review on expert reports](https://www.hofstralawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/48.1.bb_.4.greenbaum.pdf)).
- Prep focuses on the classic Daubert pressure points: the facts/data relied on, the methodology, and its application — with mock cross specifically on methodology ([ABA "Daubert-Proofing Your Expert"](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/litigation/resources/newsletters/mass-torts/daubert-proofing-your-expert/); [ForensisGroup](https://www.forensisgroup.com/resources/expert-legal-witness-blog/direct-vs-cross-examination-a-guide-to-questioning-expert-witnesses)).
- Experts may rely on data "professionals in the field reasonably rely upon," but must be able to explain which parts reflect their own judgment and how delegated work was supervised and checked ([Advocate Magazine on preparing retained experts](https://www.advocatemagazine.com/article/2017-october/preparing-your-retained-expert-witness-for-deposition)).

**Why it works.** The standard isn't "did you do every calculation" but "can you defend every calculation" — a *verification* duty rather than an *authorship* duty. The expert survives by knowing the method, spot-checking the outputs, and being able to reconstruct any number's derivation on demand.

## 8. Failure modes: what unpreparedness looks like from the bench

**Findings.**
- "There is no excuse for a lack of complete familiarity with the record, no matter how voluminous" — "I wasn't at trial" earns the retort "neither was I, but we're both here now" ([JAMS, "How Not to Argue an Appeal: A Former Judge's View"](https://www.jamsadr.com/blog/2022/how-not-to-argue-an-appeal-a-former-judges-view)).
- The killer question is provenance: an advocate interrupted seconds in with *"Where is that in the record?"* — and having no answer ([ABA, "How to Confront Your Worst Fears about Appellate Oral Argument"](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/litigation/committees/appellate-practice/articles/2018/winter2018-how-to-confront-your-worst-fears-about-appellate-oral-argument/)).
- The cardinal sins are **evasion** (not answering the question asked) and **misstating the record** — one confident false citation destroys credibility for everything else ([Hawaii practitioner rules on appellate advocacy](https://www.hccw.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Some-Rules-Comments-And-Observations-On-The-Art-Of-Appellate-Oral-Advocacy.pdf)).

**Why this matters.** Challengers don't test everything; they test *one thing deeply*. The difference between prepared and unprepared is not breadth of fluency but whether any randomly chosen thread can be pulled to its source without snapping.

## Transferable principles for defending AI-generated content

1. **Verification duty, not authorship duty (from expert witnesses / 30(b)(6)).** No one demands the presenter did the work — they demand the presenter can *trace and defend* it. Build an explicit adoption step: before presenting AI output, the human reviews it well enough to explain how any claim was derived and signs off. "Adoption by signature" without review is the recognised malpractice.
2. **The brief is an interrogation-ready artifact (from brief to counsel).** Package AI output the way solicitors package for barristers: a narrative summary, a chronology/derivation trail, numbered sources for every claim, known weaknesses flagged, and open questions listed. Design the handover for *lookup under pressure*, not linear reading.
3. **Prepare 10× the questions you'll get; drill in shuffled order (from SCOTUS prep).** Generate the full space of hostile questions (AI is ideal for this), draft answer-first responses, and rehearse entering the material at random points — Roberts' shuffled index cards — so command doesn't depend on the presentation's sequence.
4. **Moot it before it counts (from moot courts / murder boards).** A cheap dress rehearsal in front of colleagues playing hostile questioners, followed by candid critique, finds the weak claims before the real audience does. Two or three short moots beat one long read-through.
5. **Separate format-drill from content-drill (from R v Momodou familiarisation).** Train people on *handling hostile questioning as a skill* — pausing, answering only what's asked, bridging — separately from any specific deck, so on the day their attention goes entirely to substance. Answers must remain authentically their own or they crack.
6. **Make "I don't know — I'll check" a trained, legitimate move (from deposition rules).** The single biggest failure is fabricating fluency. Teach the golden rules: listen fully, pause, answer only the question, never guess, and treat honest uncertainty plus a commitment to follow up as credibility-*enhancing*.
7. **Scope the exposure (from 30(b)(6) noticed topics and the apex doctrine).** Bound in advance what the presenter is answerable for, and prepare to full depth *within that boundary*. Declare what's outside it and who owns it — a defensible perimeter beats shallow universal coverage.
8. **Provenance is the killer question (from appellate failure modes).** "Where is that in the record?" is the AI-era question too: "where did that number come from?" Every claim in AI-generated content needs a one-hop path to its source, and one confidently false citation poisons trust in everything else — so verify the load-bearing claims first.
